Essay

The visible trace of thought

A protocol for evaluating the process, not the product. How to read the conversation between student and machine as a document of thought.

Read

There is a precise moment when the question of academic evaluation changes its nature. It arrives when one realises that the text submitted by a student could have been generated by an artificial intelligence, and that there is no longer any reliable way to distinguish it from a text written entirely by the student's own hand. At that point, the problem ceases to be technical and becomes philosophical: if the machine can produce a good text, where has the thinking gone? What, exactly, are we evaluating when we evaluate?

This essay is born from the conviction that the answer lies neither in prohibiting the use of artificial intelligence nor in attempting to unmask it with detection tools that are already structurally inadequate. The answer lies in radically changing the object of evaluation: from product to process, from the finished text to the conversation that generated it. When a student works with a language model, their thinking does not disappear: it shifts. It is deposited in the prompts they formulate, in the corrections they make, in the resistance they oppose to the ease of the automatic response, in their ability to recognise when the machine is producing something generic and to force it to do better, or to abandon its proposal in favour of their own intuition.

Artificial intelligence has not eliminated the need to think. It has made thought, for those who exercise it, visible as never before.

The Process Evaluation Protocol proposed in this essay is a tool built to read that conversation as a document of thought. Five evaluation axes, each with its own specific weight, each with four levels describing as many qualities of the interaction between the student's mind and the machine's mind. The underlying idea is that the conversation log – the entire sequence of prompts, responses, revisions, abandonments – is a text in every sense: a text that tells how the student thought, not just what they produced. And that this text, for those who know how to read it, is more revealing than any final paper.

The protocol does not claim to solve all the problems of evaluation in the age of artificial intelligence. It acknowledges its limits – of scalability, of disciplinary coverage, of teacher training – and discusses them openly. What it proposes is a change of perspective: to stop searching for traces of authenticity in the product and to begin recognising the traces of thought in the process. Because it is there, in the well-posed question, in the conscious correction, in the refusal of the first answer, that thought becomes visible.

The five axes in detail

25%

Resistance to facility

The heart of the protocol. It measures the student's ability not to accept the machine's first answer, to recognise genericity and to actively seek complexity. It is the difference between those who delegate thought and those who traverse it.

Excellent

Rejects generic responses, reformulates, introduces original complexity

Good

Requests deeper analysis, compares with external sources

Sufficient

Partially modifies outputs, asks for clarifications

Insufficient

Accepts outputs without critical intervention

25%

Corrective behaviour

Evaluates the student's ability to identify errors, inaccuracies and genericity in generated responses and to intervene with specific corrections. Those who can correct a machine demonstrate that they possess the competence the machine simulates.

Excellent

Identifies specific errors, corrects with sources, improves overall quality

Good

Recognises inaccuracies, requests targeted verification

Sufficient

Intervenes on obvious errors, overlooks nuances

Insufficient

Does not identify errors, accepts everything as correct

20%

Quality of initial questioning

The quality of the question determines the quality of the answer. This axis evaluates the student's ability to formulate precise, contextualised, progressive prompts – to ask well, which is the first and most decisive act of thought.

Excellent

Precise, contextualised prompts with explicit constraints and logical progression

Good

Clear prompts with some constraints, good specificity

Sufficient

Generic but topic-relevant prompts

Insufficient

Vague prompts, without context or direction

15%

Dialogue architecture

The conversation with the machine has a structure. This axis evaluates whether that structure is chaotic or governed, whether themes develop coherently, whether there is a project behind the sequence of prompts – whether, in other words, the dialogue reveals an intention.

Excellent

Clear structure, governed thematic progression, overall coherence

Good

Good organisation, some controlled digressions

Sufficient

Recognisable structure but with frequent dispersions

Insufficient

No structure, random sequence of prompts

15%

Metacognitive transparency

The capacity to reflect on one's own process while carrying it out. This axis measures whether the student annotates their own choices, explains why they changed direction, recognises the limits of what the machine has produced – whether, in short, they are aware of what they are doing and can account for it.

Excellent

Continuous reflection, annotations on choices, awareness of limits

Good

Occasional comments on the process, some justifications

Sufficient

Rare metacognitive notes, minimal awareness

Insufficient

No reflection on the process, purely mechanical use

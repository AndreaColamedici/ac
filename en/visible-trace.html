The visible trace of thought

A protocol for evaluating process, not product. How to read the conversation between student and machine as a document of thought.

There is a precise moment when the question of school assessment changes nature. It arrives when we realize that the text submitted by the student may have been generated by an artificial intelligence, and that there is no longer any reliable way to distinguish it from a text written entirely by hand. In that moment, the problem ceases to be technical and becomes philosophical: if the machine can produce a good text, where has thinking gone? What, exactly, are we evaluating when we evaluate?

The question is not whether the student used AI, but how they used it: this shift is at the heart of the proposal.

This essay arises from the conviction that the answer lies neither in prohibiting the use of artificial intelligence, nor in attempting to unmask it with detection tools that are already structurally inadequate. The answer lies in radically changing the object of evaluation: from product to process, from the finished text to the conversation that generated it. When a student works with a language model, their thinking does not disappear: it shifts. It deposits itself in the prompts they formulate, in the corrections they make, in the resistance they offer to the ease of automatic responses, in their ability to recognize when the machine is producing something generic and to push it to do better, or to abandon its proposal and follow their own intuition.

Artificial intelligence has not eliminated the need to think. It has made thinking, for those who practice it, visible as it has never been before.

The Process Evaluation Protocol that this essay proposes is a tool built to read that conversation as a document of thought. Five evaluation axes, each with its own specific weight, each with four levels describing different qualities of interaction between the student's mind and the machine's mind. The underlying idea is that the conversation log — the entire sequence of prompts, responses, revisions, abandonments — is a text in every sense: a text that tells how the student thought, not just what they produced. And that this text, for those who know how to read it, is more revealing than any final paper.

The paradigm shift: stop looking for traces of authenticity in the product and begin recognizing traces of thought in the process.

The protocol does not claim to solve all the problems of assessment in the age of artificial intelligence. It acknowledges its limits — of scalability, disciplinary coverage, teacher training — and discusses them openly. What it proposes is a change of perspective: to stop looking for traces of authenticity in the product and to begin recognizing the traces of thought in the process. Because it is there, in the well-posed question, in the conscious correction, in the rejection of the first answer, that thought makes itself visible.
